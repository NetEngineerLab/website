Is One 10G Uplink Enough for a 24-Port Gigabit Switch?

Example only, not a measured customer network. A 24×1G switch with one uplink is a 2.4:1 line-rate design on 10G and a 24:1 design on 1G. At 30% simultaneous utilization the 10G uplink still passes an 80% planning target; the 1G uplink does not.

This question is the 24-port form of the uplink-throat problem: every access port shares one leaving link. It is not the 48×1G with 2×10G case already on this site.

Example assumptions

  • Access: 24 ports × 1 Gbps. Offered line rate = 24 Gbps.
  • Busy-hour model: 30% utilization, 100% of ports in the demand pool, 0% extra overhead.
  • Planning target: keep demand inside 80% of uplink line rate.
  • Compare one 10 Gbps uplink with one 1 Gbps uplink. No second path is modeled.

Steps

  1. Demand = 24 × 1 Gbps × 0.30 = 7.2 Gbps.
  2. 10G uplink: capacity 10 Gbps, safe capacity 8.0 Gbps, spare 8.0 − 7.2 = 0.8 Gbps. Line-rate ratio 24 / 10 = 2.4:1. Status pass.
  3. 1G uplink: capacity 1 Gbps, safe capacity 0.8 Gbps, spare 0.8 − 7.2 = -6.4 Gbps. Line-rate ratio 24 / 1 = 24:1. The same demand needs 9 one-gigabit uplinks at this target. Status fail.

What the result does and does not say

One 10G uplink can carry this 7.2 Gbps example. It is still a single forwarding path: a cut or optic failure removes the whole 10 Gbps. Do not treat the pass result as N-1 approval. A 1G uplink, or a 10G port that negotiates down to 1G, fails the same demand. LAG hashing is outside this example because there is only one member.

Limits and sources

Units are Gbps of line rate, not application goodput. The 30% figure is an example input, not a survey. Public discussion of the 24-port 1G-versus-10G throat is in vendor and design notes; this page recalculates that question with the site engine rather than copying their traffic claims.

Sources: Cisco, 25GE and 100GE campus oversubscription.
