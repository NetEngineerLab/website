Can One 10G Uplink Carry 24 Ports of 2.5G Access?

Example only. Twenty-four 2.5 Gbps access ports are 60 Gbps of line rate. One 10 Gbps uplink is a 6:1 design. At 20% simultaneous utilization the modeled demand is already 12 Gbps, above both the 10 Gbps link and the 8 Gbps safe target.

Faster access moves the constraint onto the uplink. A 48×2.5G ratio line already appears in the 48-port guide. This example is the smaller, single-uplink case: 24 multigig ports and one 10G leaving link, which is the shape used when Wi-Fi 6/7 access points negotiate 2.5G.

Example assumptions

  • Access: 24 × 2.5 Gbps = 60 Gbps offered.
  • Utilization 20%, concurrency 100%, overhead 0%, target 80%.
  • One active 10 Gbps uplink. Failure of that uplink leaves no forwarding path in the model.

Steps

  1. Line-rate ratio = 60 / 10 = 6:1.
  2. Demand = 60 × 0.20 = 12 Gbps.
  3. Safe capacity = 10 × 0.80 = 8 Gbps. Spare = 8 − 12 = -4 Gbps.
  4. The engine status is fail, with congestion and a single-path warning. Meeting the 80% target needs 2 ten-gigabit uplinks for this demand, and those two links are still not an N-1 design by themselves.

Boundary

Local switching that never leaves the access switch is not in the 12 Gbps figure. If you can show that part of the traffic stays on the switch, reduce the demand and rerun the calculator. Do not keep the 6:1 headline and silently drop the busy-hour check.

Source: Duxbury Networking, the uplink bottleneck when access moves to 2.5 Gbps. Their article states the 6:1 ratio; the 12 Gbps demand is this example, not their measurement.
