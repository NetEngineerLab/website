How to Calculate Oversubscription for a 48-Port Switch

Use a 48-port switch as a practical example to compare 2×10G, 4×10G, 2×25G and N-1 uplink capacity.

48-Port SwitchUplink SizingN-1 ResiliencePractical Example

For a 48-port switch, oversubscription is calculated by dividing total downlink capacity by total usable uplink capacity.

Oversubscription Ratio = Total Downlink Capacity ÷ Total Uplink Capacity

Quick Answer: 48 × 1G with 2 × 10G Uplinks

48 × 1 Gbps = 48 Gbps downlink capacity

2 × 10 Gbps = 20 Gbps uplink capacity

48 ÷ 20 = 2.4:1

The theoretical oversubscription ratio is therefore 2.4:1. That number alone does not tell you whether the design is sufficient. You still need to examine busy-hour demand, link aggregation behavior, redundancy and growth.

Is 2 × 10G Enough for a 48-Port Gigabit Switch?

It can be enough for many user-access networks, because endpoints rarely transmit at 1 Gbps continuously. But the correct answer depends on the traffic profile rather than port count alone.

40 active ports × 1 Gbps × 25% busy-hour utilization = 10 Gbps modeled demand

2 × 10G uplinks = 20 Gbps aggregate capacity

Normal modeled headroom = 10 Gbps

In this example, normal operation has substantial modeled headroom even though the theoretical line-rate ratio is 2.4:1.

Check N-1 Before Approving the Design

If the two 10G uplinks are also part of the redundancy design, recalculate the network after one member fails.

Normal: 48 / 20 = 2.4:1

N-1: 48 / 10 = 4.8:1

If modeled peak demand is 12 Gbps, the two-link state has 8 Gbps of headroom, while the surviving single 10G uplink has a 2 Gbps deficit. The line-rate ratio did not merely get worse; the failure state may now be unable to carry expected demand.

Should You Use All 48 Ports or Only Connected Ports?

Use both views. All 48 ports represent maximum installed design capacity and future expansion. Connected ports describe the current operational condition.

32 connected 1G ports with 2 × 10G uplinks: 32 / 20 = 1.6:1

What Changes with 48 × 2.5G Access?

48 × 2.5 Gbps = 120 Gbps

2 × 25 Gbps = 50 Gbps

120 / 50 = 2.4:1

Multigigabit access can dramatically increase the demand placed on uplinks. A design that looks generous for 1G desktop access may become constrained when APs, workstations or edge devices negotiate 2.5G, 5G or 10G.

Remember the LAG Limitation

Two 10G links in a LAG can provide 20 Gbps of aggregate capacity, but not necessarily 20 Gbps to a single flow. Hashing and the number of independent flows determine how effectively member links are used.

48-Port Switch Uplink Sizing Checklist

  1. Confirm access-port count and negotiated speed.
  2. Calculate maximum installed downlink capacity.
  3. Measure active ports and busy-hour demand.
  4. Calculate aggregate active uplink capacity.
  5. Calculate line-rate oversubscription.
  6. Check LAG distribution assumptions.
  7. Recalculate the surviving N-1 topology.
  8. Add expected traffic growth.
  9. Compare realistic upgrades such as 2×10G, 4×10G and 2×25G.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the oversubscription ratio of a 48-port 1G switch with two 10G uplinks?

2.4:1, because 48 Gbps of theoretical downlink capacity is divided by 20 Gbps of aggregate uplink capacity.

What is the ratio with four 10G uplinks?

1.2:1.

What happens if one of two 10G uplinks fails?

The aggregate uplink falls from 20 Gbps to 10 Gbps, and the theoretical ratio becomes 4.8:1.

Is 2.4:1 acceptable?

It can be, but acceptance should be based on measured or modeled peak demand, redundancy, growth and workload behavior rather than the ratio alone.
