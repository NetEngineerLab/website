Does 2×10G Still Pass After 20% Growth and One Uplink Failure?

Example only. Today’s model is 12 Gbps on 48×1G at 25% utilization, with 4 Gbps of spare against an 80% target. Growing the port-equivalent demand by 20% leaves the normal 2×10G state passing and drops the single surviving 10G member to a 6.4 Gbps deficit.

The published N-1 examples on this topic use a fixed busy-hour demand. This example adds a growth step before the failure check, which is the question to ask when the closet is not full yet.

Example assumptions

  • Baseline: 48 × 1 Gbps, utilization 25%, concurrency 100%, overhead 0%, two 10 Gbps uplinks, target 80%.
  • Growth: 20% more port-equivalent demand. The engine models that as 57.6 port-equivalents, not as a purchase of a fractional switch.
  • Failure: one uplink removed. No burst percentage is added.

Steps

  1. Baseline demand = 48 × 0.25 = 12 Gbps. Safe capacity 16 Gbps. Spare 4 Gbps. Ratio 2.4:1. Pass.
  2. After 20% growth, demand = 14.4 Gbps. Normal capacity is still 20 Gbps and safe capacity 16 Gbps, so spare is 1.6 Gbps. Ratio 2.9:1. Pass.
  3. N-1 after that growth: surviving capacity 10 Gbps, safe capacity 8 Gbps, spare 8 − 14.4 = -6.4 Gbps. Ratio 5.8:1. Fail.

Boundary

A design that passes today and still passes the grown normal state can fail the failure state. Approve growth only after the N-1 row is rerun. The 57.6 figure is the engine’s growth multiplier, not a port you can order. Measured growth that stays below 14.4 Gbps can be substituted and recalculated.

Source: Cisco, assess oversubscription in normal and failure conditions.
