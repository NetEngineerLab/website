What this topic decides

This hub sizes sensible cooling for one conditioned IT room: which heat is inside the room, what design load that heat becomes, how many precision units carry it, and whether one unit can stop. It does not calculate facility PUE. Whole-site compute, power and cooling together stay on the data center capacity topic.

  • How does a design load in kilowatts become refrigeration tons and a unit count?
  • Does a normal-state unit count still pass after one unit stops?
  • How do a smaller temperature rise and local dry-air density change the airflow estimate without changing the kilowatt capacity?

Principle

Count heat only where it is released. IT electrical power that stays in the room becomes sensible heat. UPS and PDU losses count only when that heat is released in the same room. Lighting, people, envelope gain and other sensible heat follow the same boundary. A margin is a documented allowance on that sum, not a replacement for the inventory.

Nameplate tons are not the design load. Select on sensible capacity at the expected return-air condition. N+1 compares the design load with the sensible capacity that remains after one unit is unavailable. Passing the normal state does not answer that check.

Airflow is the volume used to carry the design heat across a stated temperature rise. With altitude left blank, the cooling calculator keeps the 1.2 kg/m³ reference. An entered altitude rescales only the local airflow with dry-air density. The unit count still follows the entered kilowatts. That correction is not a cooling-capacity derating.

PUE divides data-center electrical energy by IT equipment energy for one period and one boundary. Cooling tons do not enter that ratio. Cooling electricity does, when it is inside the facility total and outside the IT meter. The definition, the formula and an assumed example are in the PUE calculation guide linked below.

Field case: 100 kW IT, five 30 kW units

IT 100 kW, UPS loss 8 kW, PDU loss 4 kW, lighting 2 kW, four people at 120 W, envelope 3 kW and other sensible heat 1 kW. Base sensible load is 118.48 kW. With 15% headroom the design load is 136.25 kW, about 38.74 tons. Five units at 30 kW sensible give 150 kW and cover the normal state. After one unit stops, 120 kW is below 136.25 kW, so N+1 fails. The cooling calculator is the check; do not treat nameplate tons as the design load.

Recommended path

  1. Define the cooling boundary and heat sources.
  2. Calculate base and design sensible load.
  3. Convert to tons and select on usable sensible capacity.
  4. Verify N+1 and airflow distribution.

Formula and method

Base sensible load = IT + in-room UPS loss + in-room PDU loss + lighting + people + envelope + other sensible heat.

Design load = base sensible load × (1 + engineering margin).

Refrigeration tons = design kilowatts ÷ 3.51685. The hall case on this page follows that path: base 118.48 kW, 15% margin, design 136.25 kW, about 38.74 tons.

Running units are the design load divided by one unit's sensible capacity, rounded up. N+1 then removes one unit and compares what remains with the design load. Reference airflow uses the design heat, 1.2 kg/m³, the specific heat of air and the temperature rise. Local dry-air density changes that airflow figure only.

Frequently asked questions

Does dry-air density reduce cooling capacity?

No. In this calculator it changes the local airflow estimate. The unit count still follows the entered sensible kilowatts.

Does a lower PUE mean the room has enough cooling?

No. PUE is an electrical-energy ratio for a stated period and boundary. Room capacity is the sensible-load and N+1 check on this topic.

Can IT power be used directly as cooling capacity?

IT power is the main heat source, but in-boundary electrical losses and room gains still need to be added with a justified margin.

Why verify N+1?

A normal-state capacity pass does not prove the room remains controlled after one unit is unavailable.
