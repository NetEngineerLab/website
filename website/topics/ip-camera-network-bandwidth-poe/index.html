Camera network bandwidth and PoE switch power budget

Camera network bandwidth is the sum of concurrent streams on one path, in Mbit/s, times a stated overhead allowance. The PoE switch power budget is a separate watt check and does not set the bitrate.

What this topic is for

This hub is for an access switch that both powers cameras and carries their video. It keeps three answers apart: bitrate in Mbit/s, switch watts, and voltage at the camera. The figures below are scenario examples, not a universal camera bitrate.

Which question you are asking

  • How much data is on one path? Count only the streams that use that path, then read the throughput guide.
  • Does the switch power budget cover those cameras? Use the PoE power budget calculator. It returns watts, not Mbit/s.
  • Is the voltage at the camera still above the floor you entered? Use the voltage-drop calculator. A watt pass does not answer it.

Why the three answers stay separate

Bitrate, watts and voltage do not share a formula. Sections 1–7 are the method: what to add, what not to count twice, and which calculator owns which unit. A result from one section is not an input the others invent.

1. Bandwidth formula

Path payload (Mbit/s) = the sum of streams that use that path at the same time. If every camera contributes the same streams, payload = count × the sum of those per-camera rates. Planning load = payload × an overhead allowance. This site’s worked allowance is × 1.15. It covers headers and short bursts when the input is an encoded bitrate. It is not an IEEE requirement. Do not apply it again to a switch-port measurement that already includes headers.

2. Count times one rate

One example stream of 4.0 Mbit/s, not a datasheet value: 100 cameras give 400 Mbit/s and 460 Mbit/s after × 1.15; 300 cameras give 1200 and 1380 Mbit/s; 500 cameras give 2000 and 2300 Mbit/s. That is the path those cameras share, not automatically every access uplink.

3. H.264 and H.265

H.264 (ITU-T H.264) and H.265 (ITU-T H.265) are different standards. Do not apply a fixed savings percentage. Axis’s bitrate-control white paper ties variable bitrate to scene activity and does not give one percent for every camera. The on-site planner still multiplies megapixels by 0.075 (H.264), 0.045 (H.265) or 0.24 (MJPEG), then by (frames per second ÷ 15) × 8. Those coefficients are an internal placeholder. Do not reuse the gap between 0.075 and 0.045 as a savings percent. The planner is not modified here.

4. Main and sub streams

Add a stream only when it is enabled and it uses the path. Example, not a datasheet: main 4.0 Mbit/s to an upstream NVR and sub 0.5 Mbit/s to an upstream operator PC, both unicast and concurrent, is 4.5 Mbit/s per camera on the uplink. If the operator PC is on the same access switch, the sub stream is not on the uplink. Do not count the same main stream again on the aggregation link.

5. Switch uplink

Size the access uplink for the cameras on that switch. The planner’s own line is its estimated total × 1.20, then the smallest of 1, 10, 25, 40 or 100 Gbit/s that is at least twice that total. That sketch is not this guide’s × 1.15, and it does not prove the two links are independent. Do not stack 1.15 on a planner result.

6. Redundancy and margin

N-1 for bandwidth means the surviving path carries the load that moves onto it. One uplink has no surviving path. Growth × 1.20 in the worked case is a project choice applied after overhead, not a standard. PoE watt headroom does not change Mbit/s.

7. PoE power is separate

The PoE power budget calculator uses source watts = PD watts ÷ (1 − cable-loss fraction), then required budget = source watts × count × (1 + headroom). It also checks port count and the class PD limits 12.95, 25.5, 51 and 71.3 W. The planner’s watt line is count × watts × 1.25. Those are different models. Remote voltage is a third model in the voltage-drop calculator.

8. Example: 16 cameras

Example. Sixteen cameras, main 4.0 and sub 0.5 both leaving the switch: payload 72.0 Mbit/s, × 1.15 = 82.8 Mbit/s, × 1.20 growth = 99.36 Mbit/s. If the sub stream stays on the switch: 64.0, then 73.6, then 88.32 Mbit/s. Recorded main stream only, decimal units: 43.2 GB/day each, 691.2 GB/day for 16, 20.736 TB in 30 days. Same 16 cameras at 12 W, 15% loss, 20% headroom, 802.3at: 271.06 W. Twenty-four cameras: 406.59 W. A 370 W switch passes 16 and fails 24 on watts. The planner converts 1 Mbit/s for one day into 0.0108 decimal TB and folds its 20% allowance into that storage line. The 90 m voltage example is a different load, 25.5 W, and returns 37.87 V on 26 AWG and 43.51 V on 23 AWG against an entered 42.5 V floor.

10. Common mistakes

  • Reading bandwidth from the resolution label, or from a fixed H.265 percent.
  • Adding a sub stream that does not use the uplink, or dropping one that does.
  • Counting one stream twice, or applying both 1.15 and the planner’s 1.20.
  • Treating a PoE watt pass as bitrate or as far-end voltage.
  • Calling N-1 passed on a switch with one uplink.

If the picture stalls, measure one port and list each stream’s destination. If cameras reboot when infrared starts, check watts and voltage.

Frequently asked questions

Can resolution alone predict bandwidth for IP cameras?

No. Codec, frame rate, scene motion and the bitrate setting change the result. Use the configured or measured average bitrate for each stream.

Does a PoE power budget calculator give camera network bandwidth?

No. The PoE power budget calculator returns watts, port count and a class limit. Camera network bandwidth is a separate sum of concurrent stream bitrates in Mbit/s.

Can I subtract a fixed percentage when moving from H.264 to H.265?

No. Do not apply a fixed savings percentage. H.264 and H.265 are different standards, and the bitrate still depends on the scene and the encoder settings.
