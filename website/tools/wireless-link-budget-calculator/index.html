Engineering notes

    Point-to-point wireless link budget and reliability planning

    Use this calculator for 2.4, 5, 6, 11, 24 or 60 GHz line-of-sight planning. It combines radio power, feeder and antenna data with free-space loss, a manual additional-loss allowance and a single-position first Fresnel-zone check.

    EIRP, FSPL and received signal

    EIRP is transmit power minus transmit feeder loss plus transmit antenna gain. The engine then subtracts free-space path loss, manual additional loss and receive feeder loss, and adds receive antenna gain.

    Pr = Pt − Ltx + Gtx − FSPL − Lextra + Grx − Lrx

    FSPL describes unobstructed free space; it is not a complete terrain, diffraction, rain, gas, foliage or interference model.

    Fade margin and target availability

    Fade margin is received level minus the entered receiver sensitivity. The 10 dB and 20 dB status bands are planning screens only. Required margin depends on the target modulation, availability, geoclimate, multipath and precipitation statistics.

    Fresnel clearance and path profile

    The tool calculates the first Fresnel radius at one obstacle position and compares actual clearance with 60% of that radius. A route survey must still model the full terrain profile, Earth curvature, refraction, structures and vegetation growth.

    High-frequency loss and regulatory review

    Rain and atmospheric gases become important on many microwave and millimetre-wave paths; near 60 GHz, oxygen absorption must be entered as additional loss. Computed EIRP is not a compliance decision—check local band, power, antenna and licensing rules.

    Wireless link budget FAQ

    How much fade margin does a point-to-point wireless link need?

    There is no universal margin. Choose it from the target availability, frequency, path length, climate, terrain and the receiver threshold for the required modulation and throughput. The 10 dB and 20 dB badges are screening levels, not availability guarantees.

    Does additional loss automatically calculate rain, oxygen or foliage attenuation?

    No. It is a manual combined allowance. Estimate rain, atmospheric gas, foliage, radome, polarization, alignment and other losses with an applicable model or measurement, then enter their total here.

    Does the EIRP result prove that the radio is legal to operate?

    No. The tool only adds transmit power and antenna gain and subtracts transmit-side loss. Check the band, channel, bandwidth, antenna rules, licensing and EIRP or power limits for every jurisdiction and link endpoint.

    Does 60% Fresnel clearance prove the entire path is clear?

    No. The radius is calculated only at the entered obstacle position. A complete path profile must include terrain, buildings, future vegetation, Earth curvature and atmospheric refraction along the whole route.

    Which receiver sensitivity should be used?

    Use the vendor threshold for the modulation, coding rate, channel width and throughput you intend to sustain, not merely the most sensitive low-rate value. Also verify polarization, antenna alignment and interference on site.

    Propagation engineering references

    • ITU-R P.525: calculation of free-space attenuation
    • ITU-R P.530: terrestrial line-of-sight propagation and availability
    • ITU-R P.676: attenuation by atmospheric gases
    • ITU-R P.526: diffraction, terrain and obstacles

    Confirm the design with current equipment data, a terrain profile, spectrum measurements and an on-site alignment and throughput test.
