How it’s calculated · methods & formulas
fiber length = route distance × route factor
propagation = fiber length × n_g ÷ c
one-way = propagation + equipment + serialization
RTT = 2 × one-way

How to use

  1. Enter the route distance in km or miles, or the measured fiber length (then keep route factor 1.0).
  2. Set a route factor if you only know the straight-line distance.
  3. Choose the fiber group index (1.468 is a good default for standard single-mode fiber).
  4. Optionally add active devices and their per-device delay from datasheets.
  5. Optionally set packet size and line rate to include serialization delay.
  6. Read one-way latency, RTT and the comparison with vacuum, microwave and copper.

Propagation delay

Propagation delay = fiber length × group index ÷ c. With c = 299,792.458 km/s and n_g = 1.468, light covers about 204,000 km/s in glass, i.e. 4.897 µs per km. The group index (not the phase refractive index) governs how fast data pulses travel; for G.652 fiber it is about 1.4677 at 1310 nm and 1.4682 at 1550 nm, so wavelength choice changes latency by less than 0.05%. Real routes are rarely straight; ducts follow roads and rail, so the fiber path is usually longer than the map distance.

Equipment and serialization

Serialization delay is the time to clock a frame onto the wire (bits ÷ line rate) and is added at each store-and-forward hop. 1500 bytes is 1.2 µs at 10 Gbps, 12 µs at 1 Gbps and 0.12 µs at 100 Gbps. Queuing and processing delays depend on load and are not modelled.

Worked case: 80 km DCI and 1,000 km × 1.3

80 km, n_g 1.468, factor 1.0, 2 devices × 5 µs and 1500 bytes at 10 Gbps × 1 hop: propagation 391.74 µs, equipment 10 µs, serialization 1.2 µs, one-way 402.94 µs, RTT 805.88 µs. A 1,000 km city pair with route factor 1.3 is 1,300 km of fiber: 6.366 ms one-way and 12.731 ms RTT from propagation alone. Straight-line vacuum over 1,000 km would be 3.336 ms one-way. Rule of thumb: a 1 ms RTT budget is about 102 km of fiber (1000 µs ÷ 9.793 µs/km).

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What is the latency of fiber per km?

About 4.9 µs one-way per km in standard single-mode fiber (group index about 1.468), or about 9.8 µs round trip — roughly 5 ms per 1,000 km one-way.

How do I calculate RTT over fiber?

RTT is about 2 × fiber length × 4.9 µs/km, plus equipment and serialization delay in each direction. 100 km of fiber gives about 0.98 ms RTT from propagation alone.

Why is my measured ping higher than the calculator?

Ping includes routing detours, equipment processing, queuing and host delay, and fiber routes are often longer than the straight-line distance. Use measured fiber length or a route factor.

Is microwave faster than fiber?

For propagation, yes. Radio through air travels at almost the speed of light in vacuum (about 3.34 µs/km), roughly 32% less delay than glass fiber.

Does wavelength change fiber latency?

Only marginally. The group index of G.652 fiber at 1310 nm and 1550 nm differs by about 0.0005, which is under 0.05% of the delay.

Is copper faster than fiber?

Not meaningfully. Twisted-pair copper propagates at about 0.65–0.70 c (about 4.8–5.1 µs/km), similar to fiber, but copper Ethernet is limited to 100 m.

Formula basis

Fiber length = route distance × route factor. Propagation = fiber length × group index ÷ c, with c = 299,792.458 km/s. One-way latency adds equipment delay and store-and-forward serialization. RTT is twice the one-way total on a symmetric path. The distance table uses n_g = 1.468 and propagation only.

Applicability limits

Planning estimate for metro, DCI and long-haul fiber delay. Asymmetric paths, queuing and host-stack delay are not included. Not a substitute for OTDR length, vendor delay datasheets, or measured RTT.
